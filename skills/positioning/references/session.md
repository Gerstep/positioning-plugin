# The 15-minute session

One founder, alone. Gut answers. If other founders will run this too, they must
not see this founder's answers first — independent answers are the whole point
of compile.

Phase budgets assume a 15-minute total. Keep momentum: if the founder
deliberates past a minute on a question, take their leaning, mark it
`low-confidence` in the answers file, and move on.

## Phase 0 — Setup (minutes 0–2)

Ask in one round:

1. Company name, your name, your role.
2. What do you do, in 2–3 plain sentences?
3. Stage and team size.
4. A URL, deck, or doc worth reading (optional).
5. Solo, or will co-founders run this too? How many?

Then immediately:

- Launch competitive research in the background per `references/research.md`.
  Do not wait for it.
- If a URL or file was given, skim it fast — product, claimed differentiators,
  current messaging. Two minutes max, in parallel with research if possible.

## Phase 1 — Category (minutes 2–5)

**Q1. The maturity test.** "When your ideal buyer goes shopping for this, can
they name the obvious choice?" Options:

- They name a clear leader, probably a number two — mature category.
- They name a few players but no de facto choice — forming category.
- They can't name anyone; they don't know what to ask for — unformed, white space.

Maturity is the number of credible players and the degree of competition — not
how many people have heard of the space.

**Q2. The path.** Route by Q1 but present all three honestly, cost included:

- **Compete** in the existing category — "we're the better version of the thing
  you already buy." Zero education cost; you fight on the leader's scoreboard.
  Categories converge to a two-horse race, so a versus-position can raise both
  boats: being the named challenger to a big brand buys you their mindshare.
- **Reframe** the category — "you're buying the right thing but judging it by
  the wrong standard." Keeps the no-education advantage and escapes the
  leader's scoreboard, but demands real discipline: you must give something up
  and will lose some deals by design.
- **Create** a new category — "the thing you're trying to buy doesn't exist
  yet." Largest ceiling, heaviest education tax. The fastest way to never
  achieve lift if the timing or the framing is off.

Then one path-specific follow-up:

- Compete → **Who is the leader, and why are they unfit to lead?** Specific —
  not "we have better tech".
- Reframe → **What is the new axis?** What does "good" actually mean that the
  current standard misses — and what do you give up by optimizing for it?
- Create → two checks, both mandatory:
  1. **Pick your fight.** Category creators polarize with the status quo.
     Which existing category are you declaring insufficient?
  2. **Category of one is failure.** Name 2–3 players you would *want* inside
     your category within a year. If nobody else could join, it's not a
     category — it's a feature.

For the create path, record the narrative arc for the pack: the world changed →
there's a new problem → no existing category suffices → our aha → the solution
→ we're the first but not the last → this is the next big thing.

## Phase 2 — Community (minutes 5–7)

**Q3. Speaking to / ignoring.** Two parts:

- Who exactly are you speaking to? A buyer and a segment, not "companies".
- Who are you *intentionally* ignoring? The no is a deeper yes to the person
  you actually serve. Do not accept "everyone else" — get a named segment they
  could plausibly serve but won't.

**Q3b. The pressure probe.** "Which off-profile deal or user are you chasing
*right now* under growth pressure that your own answer just ruled out?" Every
team has one. Naming it is the point; it goes in the pack.

## Phase 3 — The logical spine (minutes 7–10)

**Q4. What is it?** One sentence, in category words a buyer would use. Usually
derivable from Phase 1 — confirm rather than re-ask.

**Q5. Why does it matter — the So-what drill.** Take their claimed benefit and
ask "so what?" up to three times, until they hit a wall they cannot go past.
The wall is the value proposition. Record the chain, not just the wall.

**Q6. Why you, and why now?**

- Why you and not an alternative — the differentiated edge that is *relevant*.
  A differentiator nobody cares about is not a differentiator.
- Why now — what changed in the world that makes this urgent? This is the
  irreversible moment: the one-way door the industry just walked through. It
  anchors the worldview, and for a category creator it is where the story
  starts.

## Phase 4 — Statement and mind virus (minutes 10–12)

Research joins here if it is back. Show a compact picture: the 3–5 closest
players and the word each one owns, what ALL of them say (table stakes —
banned from the statement), what NONE of them say (white space), and 1–2
proposed axes where this company can be the most extreme. Ask "anything wrong
or missing?" once, briefly. If research is not back, continue; fold it in at
compile.

**Q7. The statement.** Template depends on path:

- Compete / Reframe: **"We are the ONLY [category] that [delivers X], unlike
  [competitor], which only [delivers Y]."**
- Create: **"We are the FIRST [category name] that [delivers X]."** First has
  a shelf life: when others join and someone starts redefining "best", drop
  it. Note this in the pack.

The founder writes it — gut, under 30 words. Then two checks:

- **Status arbitrage.** The competitor you name is on stage with you. Never
  elevate a low-status brand by naming it. If the named competitor is beneath
  the position, pick a bigger enemy — or name the old way of doing things
  instead of a company.
- **Constriction.** The "unlike" clause should constrict the competitor's
  value proposition to the one thing they are limited to. Fair, specific,
  reductive.

**Q8. The mind virus.** "What is the single word or phrase your brand should
own — sticky enough that the market repeats it without you in the room?" Two
tests:

- Would a customer, partner, or podcaster actually *say* it unprompted? If
  only you say it, the market reads it as suspect.
- Is there an existing term you should be **first in line** for instead of
  coining your own? A neutral term someone else coined that is becoming a
  market beats a clever term nobody adopts.

Offer 3–4 candidates generated from their answers and the research white
space; let them pick or write their own.

## Phase 5 — Feel and proof (minutes 12–15)

**Q9. Archetype.** Run the two-step picker in `references/archetypes.md`
(60–90 seconds). Output: one archetype plus a one-line voice instruction.

**Q10. The 90-day window of proof.** The market's default is disbelief. A
launch needs only a sliver of proof, but the 90 days after must compound it —
rolling thunder, not one bang. Ask for **three proofs**, each with: what
ships, what it proves, target date, announcement type (customer, product,
fundraise, data, partner). Sequence them so each is louder than the last.

**The skeptic pass — do not skip.** Play the buyer who fact-checks. For each
factual claim in the statement and key messages, check against what is
publicly visible: their site, the research findings. Two verdicts:

- **Survives** — publicly verifiable today.
- **Bet** — rewrite as "By [date], ..." and add it to the 90-day plan.

Never delete a claim silently; show the founder every rewrite.

## Output

Write `positioning/answers-<founder-name>.md` from
`assets/answers-template.md` — always, even solo.

Then:

- **Solo founder:** generate `positioning/positioning.md` from
  `assets/pack-template.md` now. Save research, if any, to
  `positioning/research.md` with source links and a retrieval date.
- **Team:** say: "Your answers are saved. When each co-founder has run
  `/positioning`, any of you runs it once more and it will compile." Do not
  show a partial pack.

Close by telling the founder their verbatim statement is preserved and will be
shown next to the final version at compile.
